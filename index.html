Why this exists

Chemsex is everywhere. The support isn't.

South Africa released its first national harm reduction guidelines for chemsex in 2025. That was a landmark moment. But guidelines on paper don't reach people in a Soweto chemsex house at 2am.

What we know

The data is thin. The signals are not.

South Africa has not yet fully quantified chemsex. That absence of data is itself part of the problem.

Available evidence paints a picture that is consistent across every study. Chemsex in South Africa is concentrated among urban MSM communities, including townships, with higher usage in Cape Town and Johannesburg. A seven-city survey found that 11% of MSM in Cape Town and 4% of MSM in Johannesburg recently used crystal meth.

In broader surveys of gay, bisexual and other men who have sex with men (GBMSM), chemsex practices have been reported in up to a third of respondents. Data from low- and middle-income countries suggest past-year chemsex prevalence ranges from 5.0% to 28.4%.

Among men who engage in chemsex in South African samples, methamphetamine is the most widely used substance, often introduced through social networks.

Chemsex is not always problematic, but some people may develop health and/or social issues with this practice.

South African harm reduction guideline for chemsex, 2025

The local context

Chemsex in South Africa doesn't look like chemsex in London or Melbourne.

Most international harm reduction resources assume a middle-class, online, private-venue model. South Africa has that too. But it also has something else entirely.

In the Global North, chemsex is often organised around online hookup sites frequented by middle-class MSM. In South Africa, township chemsex houses operate differently. In Soweto alone, OUT LGBT Well-being has worked with 17 houses where groups of MSM informally live, and where sexualised drug use is available to MSM from the broader community.

Between January 2024 and March 2025, 1,662 township-based chemsex clients received services from OUT's Engage Men's Health programme. The majority are young, aged 20–34. The profile of men in these houses is stark: 62% had not completed secondary education, and only 9% were employed.

The dual reality

URBAN MIDDLE-CLASS

Online networks

Private parties, sex-on-site venues. Accessed via Grindr and similar apps. Often invisible to public health services.

TOWNSHIP CHEMSEX HOUSES

Offline, shared spaces

Substances exchanged for sex, money, or both, for extended periods of days or weeks. Often connected to taverns.

Both need harm reduction. A single model won't reach either.

What it does to people

HIV, psychosis, suicide, violence. None of this is hypothetical.

The health consequences of chemsex in South Africa are not theoretical risks. They are documented and measured.

HIV and STI risk

HIV prevalence among MSM engaged in chemsex is high — up to nearly 40% in some samples — and care retention is poor. In township chemsex houses specifically, HIV prevalence is around 20%, and retention on ART is only 45% for chemsex clients, compared to 70% for MSM in the programme overall.

The barriers

Criminalisation. Stigma. And a service system that wasn't built for this.

Every barrier on this page is a reason someone doesn't call, doesn't disclose, doesn't come back.

01

Criminalisation

Drug possession for personal use is a criminal offence in South Africa. There is no Good Samaritan law. This means calling for help during an overdose carries legal risk. Criminalisation is not just a policy position — it is a direct barrier to emergency care.

02

Stigma

Chemsex sits at the intersection of multiple stigmas: drug use, homosexuality, sex work, and HIV. David Nel, Executive Director of OUT LGBT Well-being, puts it plainly: "All of this happens in a society where drug use is criminalised. There are thus multiple levels of stigma which we need to break."

03

Lack of knowledge and services

The national guideline states the problem directly: "Locally, there is a lack of knowledge, services, and support for people who engage in chemsex. This exacerbates their risk of exposure to HIV and other STIs, heightens barriers to accessing comprehensive care, and intensifies potential harms."

04

Healthcare discrimination

LGBTQ+ people in South Africa face persistent institutional stigma when accessing healthcare — misgendering, invasive questioning, discrimination, and inadequate staff training all contribute to delayed healthcare-seeking behaviour and interrupted continuity of care.

05

Care retention failure

Between April and September 2022, 153 chemsex clients tested HIV-positive in the EMH programme, and 139 were initiated on ART. By September 2022, despite ART home delivery, only 75 clients were retained in care — 45% retention.

Neither existing HIV services nor drug treatment services were built with sexualised drug use in mind. As a result, chemsex users often have a very high vulnerability to HIV and STIs, but also to mental ill-health and socio-economic difficulty.

AIDS 2026 conference report

The policy gap

Guidelines are necessary. They are not sufficient.

South Africa's first chemsex harm reduction guideline is a landmark. It is also a document written for clinicians, not for the men it's meant to help.

The South African harm reduction guideline for chemsex was published in the Southern African Journal of HIV Medicine in 2025. It recommends that services should:

  • Be sensitive to sexual orientation, gender identity and expression, as well as substance use and sex work
  • Employ a sex-positive approach
  • Provide judgement-free services
  • Enable accessible prevention, testing, treatment, harm reduction, and care
  • Promote PrEP, PEP and ART
  • Offer assessment, counselling, management, and referral for substance use and mental health needs
  • Facilitate access to emergency care for overdose, intimate partner violence, and sexual assault
THE GAP
A missing layer
This is the right framework. But it is aimed at clinicians. What's missing is the layer between the guideline and the person. A clinician can't reach a 23-year-old man in a Soweto chemsex house who has never disclosed his drug use to anyone. A WhatsApp chatbot can. A story from someone who's been there can. That layer is what Night Routes is for.

This isn't aspirational. It's measurable.

Know your G.

G is not like other drugs. The gap between a good night and a medical emergency is smaller than almost anything else — often less than half a millilitre. This page shows you the shape of that gap.

First, the basics

Why G is different.

Most drugs have a forgiving curve. Take a bit more, feel a bit more. G doesn't work like that.

01 · THE CURVE

It doesn't scale

With most drugs, doubling the dose roughly doubles the effect. With G, a small increase can send you from "chatting happily" to "unconscious on the floor."

02 · THE CLOCK

It lies to you

G takes 15–30 minutes to hit (GBL hits in 5–15). That window is where most overdoses happen.

03 · THE MIX

It stacks

Alcohol, benzos, opioids, ketamine and sleep meds all slow your breathing the same way G does. Together they multiply.

Tool 04

The Call

Someone is unresponsive. You have about four minutes to make the right decisions.

OVERDOSE RESPONSE

What to do, in order

South Africa does not have a Good Samaritan law. Calling carries legal risk — but a life is worth more than a charge.

1
Check if they respond

Say their name loudly. Squeeze their shoulder firmly. If nothing, press hard on the centre of their chest with your knuckles — this is a sternum rub and it's painful enough to wake someone from normal sleep.

If they don't respond to that, treat it as an overdose.

2
Check their breathing

Tilt their head back, look at their chest. Look, listen, feel for 10 seconds. G overdose often looks like loud snoring, gurgling, or gasping — that is not normal sleep.

3
Call for help — say the word "overdose"

Call 10111 (police) or 112 (mobile emergency). Say: "Someone is unconscious and not breathing properly. I think it's a GHB overdose."

4
If they're breathing: recovery position

Lie them on their side, not their back. Bring the top knee forward. Tuck the top hand under their cheek. This stops them choking if they vomit.

5
If they're not breathing: start CPR

Put them flat on their back on a hard surface. Push hard and fast in the centre of the chest — about two pushes per second.

6
Stay. And don't do these things.

Stay with them. Never leave someone alone to "sleep it off." Never put them in a cold shower, make them drink water, slap them, or inject them with anything.

And never give them more G to "bring them back." That is a myth and it kills people.

Four things people still believe

✕"He's just sleeping it off." G sleep and G overdose look almost identical. If they can't be woken with a sternum rub, it's an overdose.
✕"I'll wait and see if he comes round." Waiting is the thing that kills people.
✕"We can't call an ambulance, there's stuff here." South Africa has no Good Samaritan law. But a life is worth more than a charge.
✓What actually works: accurate measuring with an oral syringe, never using alone, waiting a full 90 minutes before redosing, and keeping G away from alcohol and downers entirely.

Know your tik.

Tik doesn't kill you with one dose the way G can. It kills you slowly, over days, while you feel completely fine. The danger isn't the high. It's the gap between how loaded your body is and how normal you think you feel.

First, the basics

Why tik is different.

G has a narrow margin. Tik has a long half-life and a lying brain.

01 · THE STACK

It accumulates

Tik stays in your body for 10–12 hours per dose. Redosing doesn't replace the last one — it stacks on top.

02 · THE LIE

You can't feel the load

Tolerance builds fast within a single session. You feel less high, so you take more, so your body gets more loaded while your brain registers less.

03 · THE CRASH

The comedown is the risk

Suicidal thoughts, crushing depression, paranoia and psychosis all live in the crash.

Aftercare

The Crash

The comedown is where most tik-related harm actually happens.

COMEDOWN CARE

What the crash is and how to survive it

1
What the crash actually is

Tik floods your brain with dopamine and noradrenaline. When it wears off, your brain has less dopamine than it started with. That's the crash. It's chemical, not character.

2
Sleep is the only real fix

Nothing resets the crash except sleep — and often a lot of it. Don't schedule anything important for 48 hours after a session if you can avoid it.

3
Food and fluids — small and often

Sip water or electrolyte drinks constantly. Try toast, yoghurt, soup, bananas. Small amounts every hour.

4
The mood — and the thoughts that come with it

The crash brings a wave of shame, self-hatred, hopelessness and sometimes suicidal thoughts. If you're having thoughts of ending your life, call SADAG 0800 567 567 or Lifeline 0861 322 322.

5
Do not use more to fix the crash

Redosing doesn't fix the depletion — it borrows more from a brain that's already overdrawn. Plan the landing before you take off.

6
When to get medical help

Chest pain, trouble breathing, seizure, severe headache, overheating with no sweat, or fainting — get emergency help. Can't urinate for 8+ hours, paranoia, hallucinations, or thoughts of harming yourself — get help today.

Four things people still believe

✕"I'm fine, I've been up for three days before." Surviving it isn't the same as it not doing damage.
✕"I'll stop when I want to." Tik changes the part of your brain that makes that decision.
✕"Benzos will bring me down safely." They can take the edge off, but they're how people end up dependent on two things at once.
✓What actually helps: telling someone what you're doing before you start, setting a hard stop, keeping G and alcohol out of the session, and having a comedown plan.

Reading the chart

What the two lines mean.

The chart is doing one job: showing you that your felt experience and your physical state move in opposite directions the longer you go.

01 · THE PINK LINE

How you feel

Rises fast in the first hour, peaks early, then falls steadily as tolerance builds. By hour 30 you can be at a fraction of your peak high while still feeling "on it."

02 · THE CYAN LINE

What your body carries

Doesn't reset between doses. Every redose adds to the pile. It climbs for the whole session and plateaus near the top.

03 · THE SHADED GAP

The deception

The coloured space between the two lines is the amount you're underestimating your own state by. Under 15 points is manageable. Over 45 points is where emergencies happen.

Four beliefs that make the gap wider

✕"I feel fine, so I'm fine." Feeling fine is not evidence of being fine.
✕"I've done three days before and I was okay." Surviving it isn't the same as it not doing damage.
✕"If I just take a bit more I'll get back to the peak." You won't. The peak is gone.
✓What actually changes the shape: stopping earlier than you want to, eating and drinking at fixed intervals, sleeping before hour 24 if you can, and never mixing with G or alcohol.

Featured story · Written · Johannesburg

"The first time I said it out loud, I said it to a stranger."

Content note: this story discusses methamphetamine use, sexual shame, and a period of suicidal thoughts. It ends well.
I'd been using for about three years before I ever said the word "chemsex" out loud. Even then, I said it to a peer worker on WhatsApp, not to anyone I knew.

The thing nobody tells you is that the shame is not really about the drugs. It's about what the drugs let you do, and what that says about you, and who might find out. I could have told you I used. I could not have told you what I used for.

What changed for me wasn't stopping. It was sitting in a room with six other men, all of us at different points in the same story, and realising that none of them looked like the version of a "chemsex user" I had in my head. They looked like my friends.

The peer worker I spoke to first never once asked me whether I wanted to stop. She asked me what I wanted my life to look like in a year. That question did more than three years of telling myself I should quit ever did.

I'm not using much now. I'm not not-using either. I have a care plan in my notes app. I have two people I text before and after. I have a GP who knows. That's the whole story, really.

If you're reading this and you've never said it out loud — say it to one person. It doesn't have to be someone who loves you. It can be a stranger on a helpline. That's what I did. It worked.

Anonymous · Johannesburg · shared with consent · paid contributor

About

Built on lived experience. Governed by it.

Night Routes is a peer-led resource for gay, bi, queer and other MSM and transgender women in South Africa navigating chemsex.

What this is

Night Routes is a story archive and navigation service. It has three jobs:

  1. Collect and publish lived experience of chemsex in South Africa, paying contributors for their knowledge.
  2. Translate harm reduction into practical tools that show the shape of risk rather than telling people what to do.
  3. Maintain a directory of South African services that are actually LGBTQ+-aware and non-judgemental in practice.

Governance

Night Routes is governed by a paid peer advisory group — people with lived experience of chemsex, drug use, or sex work in South Africa. This group has decision-making power over editorial policy, funding priorities, and the direction of the site.

Editorial policy

  • All stories published with explicit consent, credited where the contributor chooses to be named.
  • All contributors paid for their time.
  • All stories carry content notes where they discuss overdose, psychosis, suicidal thoughts, or sexual violence.
  • Contributors can withdraw their story at any time, for any reason.
  • We don't publish dosing instructions or how-to guides.
  • We don't publish anything that identifies another person without their consent.
  • We don't frame use as aspirational, and we don't frame stopping as the only "real" option.

Context

South Africa released its first national harm reduction guidelines for chemsex in 2025, published in the Southern African Journal of HIV Medicine and endorsed by the SA HIV Clinicians Society, OUT LGBT Well-being, TB HIV Care, Anova Health and others.

Night Routes builds on this foundation by centring the voices of people who actually engage in chemsex.

Know your rights

What the law actually says.

South Africa does not have a Good Samaritan law. Calling for help during an overdose carries legal risk. This page explains what the law actually says — and what you can still do.

Drug possession for personal use is a criminal offence

Under section 5 of the Drugs and Drug Trafficking Act 140 of 1992. There is no legal protection for calling emergency services during an overdose.

What this means in practice

  • Possessing tik, G, cocaine, heroin or other substances for personal use is a criminal offence.
  • You can be arrested and prosecuted for possession.
  • There is no legal protection for calling emergency services during an overdose.
  • This is a major barrier to seeking help — fear of arrest stops people from calling for ambulances.

Cannabis exception: Adults may use and possess cannabis in private for personal consumption. This does not cover sharing with friends or using in public.

What happens when you call for help

  1. You call 10111 (police) or 112 (mobile emergency). You will speak to a call-taker. They will ask for the address and what's happened.
  2. Police and/or ambulance respond. In South Africa, police may attend drug-related calls. There is no legal protection from possession charges when you call for an overdose.
  3. You may be arrested. If police attend and find drugs, you could be charged with possession.
  4. But a life is worth more than a charge. If someone is dying, call for help.

Consent

Consent must be free, informed and ongoing. You can withdraw consent at any time. If you are intoxicated to the point of being unable to consent, you cannot legally consent.

If you have experienced sexual assault, you can call the Stop Gender Violence Helpline on 0800 150 150 (24/7) or the National Counselling Line on 0861 322 322. You do not have to report to police to access support.

In the emergency department

  • Section 27(3) of the Constitution says no one may be refused emergency medical treatment.
  • You will be treated based on your clinical need, not your drug use.
  • You do not have to tell them what you took, but it helps them treat you better if you do.
  • Hospital staff are not required to report drug use to police in most circumstances.

Get legal advice

Legal Aid South Africa: 0800 110 110
Lawyers for Human Rights: (011) 339 1960
SANPUD legal support: via sanpud.org

Testing & prevention

What you're carrying that you can't feel.

Many STIs and blood-borne viruses have no symptoms for months or years. Testing isn't about shame. It's about knowing what's actually happening in your body.

What to test for

Every three months if you're sexually active with multiple partners

  • HIV
  • Syphilis
  • Gonorrhoea (throat, rectal, urethral swabs)
  • Chlamydia (throat, rectal, urethral swabs)

Annually, or more often if you inject

  • Hepatitis C
  • Hepatitis B

Where to go in South Africa

OUT LGBT Well-being — Free HIV and sexual health treatment and prevention services to MSM in Johannesburg, Nelson Mandela Bay and Buffalo City.
WhatsApp: 087 250 2762 · hello@out.org.za

Triangle Project — Sexual health clinical care, counselling, and harm reduction in Cape Town.
(021) 712 6699 · health2@triangle.org.za

Your local clinic — Every public primary healthcare facility in South Africa offers HIV testing and PrEP.

TB HIV Care — Drop-in centres, needle syringe programmes, and HIV services across multiple provinces. tbhivcare.org

PrEP

PrEP is available free at 98% of public primary healthcare facilities in South Africa. Over 2 million people have been initiated on oral PrEP since 2016.

Drug checking

Drug checking services are not widely available in South Africa. Reagent testing kits can be purchased online, but they are not as accurate as professional drug checking.

Hep C treatment

Hepatitis C is now curable with 8–12 weeks of tablets. Treatment is available in South Africa, though access may vary by province.

What to expect at a test

  • You'll be asked some questions about your sexual history. You can refuse to answer any question.
  • Blood test for HIV, syphilis, hep B and hep C.
  • Swabs for gonorrhoea and chlamydia — throat, rectal, and urine sample.
  • Results are usually back within a week or two.
  • If you test positive for something, treatment is available and often free.

Townships & chemsex houses

Chemsex is not only a middle-class story.

In South Africa, chemsex operates in two distinct contexts. One connects online and meets in private venues. The other happens in township chemsex houses.

What chemsex houses are

OUT LGBT Well-being identified a network of chemsex houses in Soweto — informal shared spaces, often matchbox houses, where groups of MSM live and where sexualised drug use is available to the broader community. Between January 2024 and March 2025, OUT worked with 17 of these houses. Some house up to 20 people at a time. 77% of participants used methamphetamine, 40% combined it with other substances, and 15% had injected drugs.

Chemsex houses are often connected to taverns, where bartenders facilitate access. Some are transactional — people exchange sex or drugs for a place to stay, food, or money. For some, drug use also becomes a means to cope with poverty.

What's different in township contexts

Access to private space. Middle-class chemsex often happens in private homes or hotel rooms. Township chemsex happens in shared spaces where privacy is limited and where the line between using, living, and surviving is blurred.

Poverty and survival. For some, chemsex is not only about pleasure — it's about access to shelter, food, or income. Harm reduction in this context has to account for the fact that "just stopping" may not be an option when the same space provides your bed and your next meal.

Overlapping stigmas. Being MSM, using drugs, and living in a township means navigating multiple layers of stigma — from family, community, healthcare workers, and police.

Services. Most LGBTQ+-specific AOD services are based in city centres. Reaching township chemsex houses requires outreach — going to where people are, not expecting them to come to you.

What harm reduction looks like in practice

  • Peer outreach. OUT and TB HIV Care have established champions in some chemsex houses — people who live there and can provide harm reduction information, condoms, lubricant, and referrals.
  • HIV testing and ART retention. OUT's Soweto chemsex programme achieved a 40% increase in ART retention among participants by working directly with chemsex houses.
  • Naloxone access. Where opioids (including whoonga) are used, naloxone distribution through peer networks saves lives.
  • Clean injecting equipment. Needle syringe programmes run by Triangle Project and TB HIV Care can be expanded to reach township chemsex houses.

Services working in townships

OUT LGBT Well-being — Soweto chemsex programme, peer outreach, HIV testing and treatment. WhatsApp: 087 250 2762

TB HIV Care — Drop-in centres, NSPs, and outreach in townships across multiple provinces. tbhivcare.org

SANPUD — Peer-led naloxone distribution and overdose response training. sanpud.org

Safer injecting

If you're going to slam, know what you're doing.

Slamming carries specific risks that smoking or snorting don't. Vein damage, abscesses, blood-borne viruses, and higher overdose risk.

Getting clean equipment

Never share needles, syringes, spoons, filters, water, or tourniquets. Sharing any of these can transmit HIV, hepatitis B and hepatitis C. The only safe option is your own equipment, used once and disposed of properly.

Where to get free injecting equipment in South Africa

  • Triangle Project — Needle and syringe programme in Cape Town. (021) 712 6699.
  • TB HIV Care — Drop-in centres and NSPs across multiple provinces. tbhivcare.org.
  • SANPUD — Peer networks and harm reduction supplies. sanpud.org.
  • Some pharmacies — Many sell injecting equipment and provide sharps disposal.

Safer injecting practice

  • Never share. Not needles, not spoons, not water, not filters. Hep C can live on a spoon for weeks.
  • Wash your hands. And the site. Alcohol swabs before and after.
  • Rotate sites. Same vein every time = collapsed vein, and eventually you'll have nowhere left.
  • Use a new needle every time. A blunt needle causes more damage and can break off.
  • Know your dose. Slamming hits harder and faster than smoking or snorting.

Vein care

  • Drink water. Dehydration makes veins harder to find.
  • Warm the site before injecting — a warm shower, or a warm compress.
  • Use a tourniquet if you need to, but release it before you push the plunger.
  • Alternate arms and sites. Give each site time to heal.
  • If a vein feels hard, cord-like, or bruised, don't use it until it heals.

What to do if you miss

A "miss" is when the substance goes into the tissue around the vein instead of the vein itself. It hurts, it burns, and it can lead to an abscess.

  • Stop injecting immediately.
  • Apply firm pressure for several minutes.
  • Elevate the site.
  • Apply a cold compress for the first 24 hours, then warm compresses.
  • Watch for redness, swelling, heat, or increasing pain. These are signs of infection — see a doctor.

Abscesses and infections

If you have a red, hot, swollen, painful lump at an injecting site, or you have a fever, get medical attention. Abscesses can be drained at most emergency departments or by a GP. Don't try to pop or drain them yourself.

Safer sharps disposal

  • Never put used needles in the bin.
  • Use a sharps container — available free from needle exchanges and some pharmacies.
  • If you don't have a sharps container, a rigid plastic container with a screw-top lid is a temporary option. Label it clearly.
  • Return full sharps containers to your needle exchange or pharmacy for disposal.

If you're injecting G

Injecting G is far more dangerous than drinking it. The dose is much smaller, the onset is faster, and the margin for error is narrower. GBL in particular is caustic and causes tissue damage. There is no safe way to inject G, but if you're going to, use an insulin syringe, know your exact dose in millilitres, and never inject alone.

Home → Naloxone
Overdose reversal

Naloxone saves lives. It won't save every life.

Naloxone reverses opioid overdose. It doesn't touch G, doesn't touch tik, doesn't touch alcohol or benzos.

What naloxone is

Naloxone is a medicine that reverses the effects of opioids — heroin, oxycodone, fentanyl, methadone, buprenorphine, and whoonga/nyaope where it contains heroin. It works by blocking opioid receptors in the brain, rapidly restoring breathing in someone who has stopped breathing from an opioid overdose.

It works within 2–5 minutes when injected, or 4–8 minutes when given as a nasal spray. It lasts for 30–90 minutes, which may be shorter than the opioid itself — so a second dose may be needed.

Naloxone does NOT reverse G, GHB, GBL, tik, cocaine, MDMA, alcohol, or benzos.

If someone is unresponsive from one of these, naloxone will do nothing. Call for help and provide airway support.

Where to get naloxone in South Africa

  • SANPUD — Peer-led naloxone distribution and training. sanpud.org.
  • NACOSA — Overdose management programmes including naloxone distribution. nacosa.org.za.
  • Triangle Project — Harm reduction services including naloxone access in Cape Town. (021) 712 6699.
  • TB HIV Care — Drop-in centres and OST programmes. tbhivcare.org.
  • On prescription — Any doctor can prescribe naloxone. It is on South Africa's Essential Medicines List.

Naloxone is currently available by prescription only in South Africa. Community-based distribution programmes are limited but growing through SANPUD and NACOSA pilots.

How to use naloxone

Injection

  1. Draw up the full contents of the syringe.
  2. Inject into the thigh muscle (not a vein).
  3. If no response in 2–3 minutes, give a second dose.

Always: Call for help first, or have someone call while you administer. Stay with the person. Put them in the recovery position if they're breathing. If they're not breathing, start CPR.

What happens after naloxone

Naloxone puts someone into immediate withdrawal. They may wake up confused, agitated, sweaty, nauseated, and in pain. This is normal and it will pass — but it can be distressing.

They still need medical assessment, even if they seem fine. Naloxone wears off before most opioids do, so overdose can recur.

Naloxone and chemsex

If you're using G or tik, naloxone won't help. If you're using opioids in a chemsex context — which is more common than people think, especially with whoonga and nyaope — having naloxone on hand can save a life.

It's worth carrying naloxone even if you don't use opioids yourself. You might be with someone who does.

Glossary

The words people actually use.

Plain-language definitions of terms that come up in chemsex contexts. No judgement, no moralising, no euphemisms.

Chemsex
Using drugs specifically in sexual contexts, most commonly by gay, bi, queer and other MSM, and transgender women. Substances usually involved are crystal methamphetamine (tik), GHB/GBL, and mephedrone, sometimes with cocaine, ketamine, or others.
PnP
"Party and Play." A term used in dating apps and online to signal that someone wants to combine drug use with sex.
Slamming
Injecting drugs, usually crystal meth, in a sexual context.
G
Slang for GHB or GBL. GBL is an industrial solvent that converts to GHB in the body. GBL hits faster and harder, and is behind many overdoses.
GHB
Gamma-hydroxybutyrate. A depressant. Prescribed in some countries for narcolepsy. Narrow margin between effect and overdose.
GBL
Gamma-butyrolactone. A prodrug for GHB — converts to GHB in the body. Faster onset, more unpredictable dose, more dangerous.
Tik
South African slang for crystal methamphetamine. Also called crystal, meth, ice. "Tik" is the most common term in South Africa, especially in the Western Cape and township contexts.
Crystal
Another term for methamphetamine. Often used by middle-class white gay men in South Africa, while "tik" is more common in township and coloured communities.
Whoonga / Nyaope
A South African street drug, usually containing heroin, sometimes with ARVs, rat poison, or other substances. Extremely addictive and dangerous. Named differently depending on region — whoonga in KZN, nyaope in Gauteng.
Slam
A single injection of a drug.
Booty bump
Administering a drug (usually tik) rectally, by dissolving it and inserting it with a syringe without a needle. Faster onset than oral, less damaging than injecting.
Chemsex house
Informal shared spaces, often in townships, where groups of MSM live and where sexualised drug use is available. In Soweto, OUT has worked with 17 such houses.
Party pack
A package of drugs, often tik and G, sold or shared together for a session.
Care plan
A written plan for a session or period of use, including limits, emergency contacts, aftercare plans, and what to do if things go wrong.
Redose
Taking another dose of a substance during a session. With tik, redosing doesn't reset the clock — it stacks on the previous dose.
The gap
The difference between how high you feel and how loaded your body actually is. Tik hides this gap; G has a narrow version of it.
Harm reduction
Practical strategies to reduce the negative consequences of drug use, without requiring abstinence. Includes things like never using alone, using clean equipment, and knowing overdose response.
Naloxone
A medicine that reverses opioid overdose. Does not work on G, tik, alcohol or benzos.
PrEP
Pre-exposure prophylaxis. A daily pill that prevents HIV. Available free at 98% of public healthcare facilities in South Africa.
PEP
Post-exposure prophylaxis. HIV medication taken within 72 hours of potential exposure. Available at emergency departments and sexual health clinics.
Hep C
Hepatitis C. A blood-borne virus that affects the liver. Curable with 8–12 weeks of tablets. Circulates in chemsex networks when snorting or injecting equipment is shared.
Stimulant psychosis
A state of paranoia, hallucinations and delusions caused by heavy or prolonged stimulant use, especially without sleep. Common beyond 48 hours of tik use.
The crash
The comedown after stimulant use. Characterised by exhaustion, low mood, shame, and sometimes suicidal thoughts. Chemical, not character.
Needle syringe programme (NSP)
A service providing free clean injecting equipment and safe disposal. Available through Triangle Project and TB HIV Care.
Opioid substitution therapy (OST)
Medication (methadone or buprenorphine) that replaces illicit opioid use, reducing withdrawal and overdose risk.
